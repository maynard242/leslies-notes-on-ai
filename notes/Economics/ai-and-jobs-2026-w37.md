# AI and Jobs: Week 2026-W37 Evidence

The evidence on AI and jobs now answers a narrower question than the headlines ask. AI is already changing tasks, effort, and the experience of work, but it has not yet produced broad employment loss. That distinction matters because a quiet aggregate can conceal large changes inside firms, occupations, and careers.

## What the evidence says

The clearest new result is that use is widespread but shallow. In a nationally representative US worker survey, [Bick, Blandin, Deming, and Schumacher (2026)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35677) map generative AI use to detailed occupations and tasks. AI appears across many kinds of work, yet fewer than half of workers use it within most occupations and tasks. Workers doing similar jobs often make different choices. Measures of technical exposure explain some adoption, but far from all of it.

This weakens a common shortcut: treating what AI can do as a measure of what it is doing. Exposure scores describe technical possibility. Adoption measures capture actual use, shaped by access, trust, management, data rules, skills, and whether a tool fits the workflow. Platform chat logs pose a related problem. Bick and colleagues find that they tend to classify generic activities too broadly across occupations. A polished map of exposed tasks may therefore overstate the amount of work already being changed.

At the worker level, the early gains can still be large where AI fits. [Hartley, Jolevski, Melo, and Moore (2024)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5136877) report that 30.1 percent of surveyed adults had used generative AI at work by December 2024. Users said it saved substantial time on assisted tasks, although these are self-reported estimates rather than causal productivity measures. Use was also uneven: younger, more educated, and higher-income workers adopted more often. The micro evidence therefore points to real gains, but not gains shared evenly across workers or applied across the whole job.

Firm-level evidence adds a second layer. [Babina, He, and Jiang (2026)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35684) find that employment of AI-skilled workers is associated with recent productivity growth. Their proposed mechanism is organization capital: firm-specific knowledge, routines, and learning built while putting AI to work. The association appeared in recent years, not across the previous decade, and was strongest for AI-skilled jobs that helped build this capital. This suggests that productivity comes less from buying a model than from changing how the firm learns and operates. It does not by itself prove that AI caused the gains.

At the macro level, displacement remains hard to see. Using US Census business survey data, [Tabarrok (2026)](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/ai-and-employment-what-the-firms-say.html) reports that 95.7 percent of firms surveyed in late 2025 and early 2026 said AI had not changed their total employment. Among AI users, supplementing existing work was far more common than taking over a former employee task. Most adopters also reported no training, new workflow, or AI-skilled hiring. This is consistent with shallow adoption, but it is not evidence that future effects will be small. A firm can reduce hiring, reassign work, or raise output without reporting an immediate change in headcount.

The costs also appear before jobs disappear. In interviews with 21 professionals at one Danish software services company, [Alami, Paja, and Tiwari (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03456v1) find anxiety over accountability, disruption to craft identity, erosion of meaning and satisfaction, heavier cognitive and verification work, and uncertainty about future roles. These findings cannot establish prevalence beyond that case. They do show what productivity measures miss: adoption can shift risk and effort onto workers even when management sees the tool as assistance.

## What it means

The current picture is not “AI replaces jobs” or “AI leaves jobs alone.” It is task change first, organizational change second, and employment effects later or elsewhere. That sequence helps explain the gap between visible micro gains and muted macro outcomes. Time saved on one task does not automatically become economy-wide productivity. Firms must redesign workflows, assign responsibility, train people, and turn repeated use into shared knowledge. Many have barely begun.

For workers, the near-term divide may be between those who can shape AI-supported work and those who must absorb it. If some employees gain speed while others inherit checking, correction, and accountability, measured output can rise while job quality falls. Junior workers may face a further risk. If AI takes routine tasks that once served as training, firms may weaken the path by which novices become experts. That is an inference, not yet settled by these papers, but it follows directly from the combination of shallow adoption, uneven use, and disrupted craft.

For employers, adoption targets are a poor substitute for work design. Counting licenses, prompts, or self-reported usage can reward visible compliance while hiding weak fit and extra verification. Better measures would track task time, error rates, rework, worker autonomy, learning, customer outcomes, and who bears responsibility when the model is wrong.

Policy should likewise watch more than unemployment. Useful indicators include hiring by age and occupation, hours, wages, job transitions, training, task mix, and reported work quality. Support should favor adaptation: access to tools, sector-specific training, portable protections, and help for smaller firms to build the routines that turn technical access into productive use. The aim is not maximum adoption. It is better work and broadly shared gains.

## Open questions

- Does shallow adoption deepen as tools improve, or persist because trust, workflow, and accountability are the real constraints?
- Who captures the gains from time saved: workers through higher wages and better jobs, firms through margins, or customers through lower prices?
- Does AI reduce entry-level hiring and learning opportunities, or create new routes into skilled work?
- Can firms build organization capital without eroding craft, autonomy, and meaning?

The labor-market signal is still quiet, but the work itself is already moving.
