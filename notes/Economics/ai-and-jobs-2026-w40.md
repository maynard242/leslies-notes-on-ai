# AI and Jobs: Week 2026-W40 Evidence

Can AI make firms more productive while making it harder for people to get a job? This week’s evidence suggests those outcomes can coexist: work may be reorganised inside firms while opportunities to enter or move between them narrow. The question is not only how many jobs disappear, but who gets access to the jobs that remain.

## What the evidence says

The studies address different questions. Enterprise interviews describe adoption, labour-market data track outcomes, exposure indices map potential effects, and models explain mechanisms. Treating them as interchangeable would turn a useful warning into a false forecast.

At the firm level, the [International Labour Organization (2026)](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ai-adoption-chinese-enterprises-boosts-productivity-raises-concerns-about) reports interviews with 21 Chinese enterprises and a survey of 1,591 professionals. Human–AI workflows predominated. One insurer reported that 300 customer-service employees increased daily throughput from 6,000 to 15,000 issues. Yet the firms were purposively selected, productivity gains were self-reported and unverified, and most lacked systematic evaluation. These are examples of what adoption can look like, not estimates of China’s aggregate productivity or employment effects. Workers’ expectations of income losses are likewise perceptions, not observed wage changes.

The US evidence points to a quieter employment adjustment. [Ahn and Carollo (2026)](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f247923.pdf) combine household and vacancy data with occupational AI exposure and an adoption proxy drawn from job advertisements. Since LLMs emerged, workers in high-exposure, high-adoption groups experienced larger declines in job-finding and job-switching rates, alongside more changes in activities within existing jobs. The deterioration is more evident in hiring than in layoffs.

Their estimated increase in structural unemployment is about 0.1–0.2 percentage point, with considerable uncertainty. This is a model-dependent macro estimate, not a count of workers demonstrably displaced by AI. The analysis addresses cyclical conditions and adoption endogeneity, but job-advertisement measures remain imperfect proxies for actual use. The authors also note recent signs of recovery in job finding and switching. The paper supports watching hiring closely, not declaring an irreversible employment collapse.

A separate mechanism could make entry harder even when vacancies exist. [Ashlagi et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30058v1) model a hiring market in which AI lowers application costs while making written applications less informative. Employers then rely more on prior experience. Capable but inexperienced applicants lose the individual signal that might have earned them an interview.

This is a theoretical result conditional on the model’s assumptions, not evidence that AI-assisted applications always conceal ability. Its useful contribution is to identify an evaluation bottleneck: making applications cheap does not make credible assessment cheap. An inexpensive intermediate assessment can restore a route to screening for some applicants. The model does not establish that this mechanism explains the US hiring slowdown.

Exposure also differs within broad categories of workers. Linking US occupational characteristics to AI measures, [Fernandez et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21756v1) find higher LLM-related exposure in female-dominated occupations. Within those occupations, exposure extends across the skill and wage distribution, rather than concentrating mainly in better-paid work. Broader, patent-based AI exposure shows a different gender pattern. Neither measure establishes displacement or wage loss, but the distinction warns against designing support around one generic category of “AI-exposed workers”.

Finally, task automation is not the same as labour saving. [Canale (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29345v1) proposes counting verification, exceptions, correction and maintenance before concluding that governance automation reduces human work. Its experiments use synthetic projects and measure review performance, not employment or realised staff-hour savings. The practical lesson is sound but narrower than a workforce forecast: an automated task can leave substantial work elsewhere in the process.

## What it means

The strongest inference is that “augmentation versus replacement” is too crude a choice. A firm can help existing employees handle more work and still recruit fewer new ones. That would protect incumbents while weakening entry routes, without producing a wave of layoffs. The studies make this possibility worth testing; they do not establish its size across economies.

Employers should therefore measure completed work at a fixed quality standard, including the human effort needed to check and repair it. Alongside output and headcount, track recruitment, junior roles, internal moves, wages and training access. A faster workflow is not a complete account of what happened to workers.

Hiring reform deserves a place beside skills policy. Short, job-relevant work samples and structured interviews could help applicants demonstrate ability without an established employment record. These are design proposals, not proven remedies from this week’s evidence. Assessments should avoid unpaid production work, accommodate disability, and be tested for whether they predict performance rather than simply add another hurdle.

Training alone cannot solve a shortage of opportunities to demonstrate competence. Paid placements, apprenticeships and supported transitions are therefore candidates for evaluation alongside AI courses. A plausible longer-term risk is that fewer entry-level opportunities weaken the future supply of experienced workers. That is a hypothesis about a feedback loop, not an observed result here.

## Open questions

- How much of weaker hiring reflects AI rather than interest rates, sectoral adjustment or uncertainty about demand?
- Do intermediate assessments improve inexperienced applicants’ chances, or mostly transfer screening costs onto job seekers?
- When do task-level gains become higher wages, shorter hours or expanding output rather than reduced recruitment?
- How well do US occupational measures and selected Chinese enterprise experiences travel to smaller firms and other labour markets?

A credible AI jobs strategy must count not only the work machines can do, but the opportunities people still have to enter, learn and advance.
