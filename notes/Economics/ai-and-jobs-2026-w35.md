# AI and Jobs: Week 2026-W35 Evidence

AI may change work before it changes employment totals. This week’s papers ask a prior question: if occupations, surveys, education, and public debate all hide important differences within broad categories, what evidence would let us see who is gaining, who is losing, and how work is being reorganized?

## What the evidence says

The strongest labor-market evidence comes from China. [Chen et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26924v1) analyze 752.6 million job advertisements from five recruitment platforms between January 2022 and June 2026. They extract separate catalogs of 20,721 candidate requirements and 44,479 job tasks, then track them monthly across occupations, cities, wages, education levels, and employer types.

This separation matters. A job requirement determines who can enter. A task describes what the worker will do. Measures based on job titles or broad skill labels often mix the two. In the authors’ accounting example, the same title covers routine bookkeeping at one firm and regulatory reporting, tax planning, and board analysis at another. The title stays fixed while both the work and the entry bar change.

Their early findings also complicate simple claims about AI displacement. Counting occupations suggests that jobs with high exposure to language models are declining. Counting the underlying tasks suggests a smaller contraction because those tasks often move into other jobs rather than disappear. This is evidence of recomposition, not proof that AI is preserving employment. Job advertisements measure employer demand, not actual hiring, employment, wages, or worker experience. Still, the study shows why occupation-level exposure scores can overstate displacement when tasks survive elsewhere.

The other papers expose related measurement problems. [Romberg et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22582v1) propose “hybrid panels” that combine human survey respondents with language models over repeated survey waves. AI might fill planned gaps, suggest answers, or help allocate scarce survey effort toward people who are harder to reach. Crucially, the authors reject full replacement of human respondents. Their pilot and framework treat human answers as the basis for calibration and continuing validation.

This is promising infrastructure, not yet proof that synthetic responses can represent workers reliably. Language models can reproduce social desirability bias, react unpredictably to wording, and miss behavior that differs from their training data. Hybrid panels may reduce survey costs or shorten questionnaires, but only if their errors are measured across groups and over time. Otherwise, apparent efficiency could conceal the very workers whose labor-market transitions are hardest to observe.

Two further papers shift attention from tasks to power. [Poudyal (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26937v1) offers a conceptual, rather than empirical, framework for human-AI co-agency in education. Its central claim is that AI does not merely help people produce work. It can also acquire practical authority over what counts as knowledge, quality, or evidence of competence. That matters for jobs because education and assessment determine who receives credentials, which skills are recognized, and whose judgment employers trust.

[Harrington et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24767v1) compare 91 empirical papers with 28 items of public discourse concerning generative AI and Black communities. Their review finds that scholarship concentrates heavily on detecting technical bias, often reducing Black identity and experience to measurable variables. This suggests a broader warning for labor research: measuring average task exposure is not the same as understanding how AI affects particular communities, occupations, or routes into work.

Taken together, these papers do not establish a net employment effect. They show that our instruments are often too coarse for the question.

## What it means

First, job titles are becoming a weaker unit of analysis. AI acts on tasks, but employers respond by rebundling tasks, changing requirements, redesigning workflows, or moving work across occupations. Headline employment may remain stable while access to good jobs narrows through higher credential, experience, or technical requirements.

Second, displacement and exclusion should be measured separately. A worker can keep a job yet lose discretion, bargaining power, or a path to promotion. Another worker may never enter because the employer raises the qualification bar. Neither change appears clearly in a simple count of jobs “exposed to AI.”

Third, adoption policy needs better feedback loops. Governments should combine employment and wage data with frequent job-ad analysis, worker surveys, employer studies, and measures of task and requirement change. AI-assisted surveys may help, but human observations must remain the reference point, especially for smaller groups and unusual experiences that models tend to smooth away.

A further implication is speculative but important. If AI makes some tasks cheaper while credentials and human judgment become more valuable, labor markets may polarize within occupations rather than only between them. The same job title could contain a low-discretion role supervised by software and a better-paid role responsible for exceptions, verification, and accountability. Tracking titles alone would miss that split.

## Open questions

1. Do changes in advertised tasks and requirements lead to actual hiring, wage, and employment changes, or do they mainly reflect shifts in how employers write advertisements?
2. When AI-exposed tasks move across occupations, do workers move with them, or do new workers capture the opportunities?
3. Can hybrid surveys reduce cost without increasing error for workers who are poorly represented in model training data?
4. Who gains authority when AI helps assess competence: workers and teachers, employers and platforms, or the model providers that define the system?

The central labor question is no longer only how many jobs remain, but who gets into them, what work they contain, and who retains the right to judge it.
