# AI for Boards: Strategy, Value, and Governance

**Board note — August 2026**

---

This note starts from a practical judgment: risk management is not the opposite of AI value creation. For consequential AI, proportionate risk management is part of what makes value scalable.[2][4]

An organization will struggle to scale AI responsibly if it does not know which systems are in use, who owns them, what data they touch, how they fail, or who can stop them.[4] But a board that discusses only these controls is still asking half the question. It may avoid a visible mistake while missing a larger change in customers, costs, products, competition, and the shape of the business itself.

The board's larger responsibility is long-term value. Its AI task is therefore not to choose between strategy and risk. It is to determine **where the technology can materially improve or threaten how the company creates and captures value, and what conditions must hold to pursue that opportunity**. Strategy points to where the company should go. Risk management determines which paths are viable and how far the company can proceed with confidence.

Recent board guidance reflects this wider mandate.

The World Economic Forum's board toolkit says seven modules focus on strategy oversight; five other topics cover ethics, governance, risk, audit, and board responsibilities.[1]

More recent frameworks put strategic value creation first and combine it with capital allocation, operating-model change, talent, risk, and technology competency.[2][3][4]

A separate board roadmap likewise places AI within strategy, performance, talent, and governance.[5]

AI governance therefore has two inseparable jobs:

1. **Direct AI toward material, long-term value.**
2. **Create the confidence, controls, and learning needed to pursue that value at scale.**

Strategy identifies where value may lie. Governance turns intent into repeatable decisions. Risk management helps make those decisions safe enough to scale.

> **Companion note:** [AI Governance: Five Questions for the Board](/notes/ai-governance-board-note) covers the control and assurance side in detail: inventory, accountability, boundaries, monitoring, and evidence.

## 1. Start with business strategy, not AI activity

An inventory can show where AI is already in use. It cannot tell the board whether those uses matter.

AI is not a strategy by itself. It may strengthen an existing advantage, remove one, or change what customers expect. It may lower the cost of an established process without altering the business. It may also change a product, a customer relationship, or an industry's value chain so substantially that the existing strategy no longer holds.

The board should ask management to examine AI against the company's real strategic choices:

- Which customer problems could we solve materially better?
- Which products or services could improve, and which could become commodities?
- Where could AI change price, quality, speed, or cost enough to alter competition?
- Which new entrants could attack a profitable part of our value chain?
- Which proprietary assets—data, distribution, domain knowledge, workflows, or trust—become more valuable with AI?
- What is the risk of moving too slowly, not only the risk of moving too fast?

Recent principles extend the board's view beyond productivity to growth, innovation, customer satisfaction, business-model change, partnerships, and M&A.[2] Board guidance also asks management to connect AI to corporate strategy and consider the cost of inaction.[5]

The useful output is not an “AI strategy” detached from the business plan. It is a revised business strategy that states where AI changes the assumptions underneath it.

## 2. Make explicit choices about where to lead

Not every AI opportunity deserves equal ambition.

Management should tell the board where the company intends to:

- **lead** because AI could produce meaningful differentiation;
- **keep pace** because the capability will become necessary but not distinctive;
- **wait** because the technology or business case is immature; or
- **exit or deprioritize** because AI changes the economics of an activity.

One current framework uses the formulation “lead, lag, or exit” to force this choice.[4] The wording matters less than the discipline. A company that calls every use case strategic has not made a strategy.

The board should expect a small number of material bets, each connected to a customer, operating, or competitive outcome. It should also expect management to state what the company will not pursue. Scarce capital, management attention, data capacity, and change capacity all have alternative uses.

## 3. Govern a portfolio, not a parade of pilots

Experiments are useful because uncertainty is real. A large collection of pilots is not evidence of progress.

The board should see AI investment as a portfolio across three horizons:

1. **Reversible experiments** that test a specific assumption cheaply and quickly.
2. **Scaled operating changes** that redesign a material workflow or customer experience.
3. **Strategic options** that may require capability building before returns can be measured reliably.

Every material initiative should have:

- an accountable business owner;
- a strategic reason for existing;
- an expected outcome and a baseline;
- the full cost, including data, integration, model usage, training, and change;
- a test or learning objective;
- criteria to scale, redesign, pause, or stop; and
- a date for the next capital-allocation decision.

Current director guidance recommends prioritizing use cases by strategic relevance, readiness, and risk tolerance, with explicit pilot-to-production criteria and go-or-stop reviews.[3] Other frameworks stress that the investment extends beyond tools to data, infrastructure, talent, partnerships, and organizational change.[2][4]

This is where the board can add independent challenge. It can protect promising experiments from premature cuts while insisting that weak projects end. Both decisions require judgment.

## 4. Redesign work instead of adding AI to it

The practitioner guidance reviewed here recommends redesigning work rather than merely adding AI to an existing process.[3][4]

Management should examine an entire piece of work:

- what outcome the customer or employee needs;
- which steps exist only because of old information or coordination limits;
- where AI can prepare, recommend, decide, or act;
- where human judgment remains necessary;
- who handles exceptions;
- how decisions are reviewed and improved; and
- which roles, incentives, and team boundaries must change.

The board's role is to test whether management has redesigned material workflows, placed human checkpoints and exception handling deliberately, and assigned accountable ownership.[3][4]

This also changes the workforce question. The board should not reduce the discussion to how many jobs AI might remove. It should ask what work becomes possible, which skills become more valuable, how entry-level learning will change, and whether employees have enough confidence and support to adopt the new way of working.

## 5. Build capability that compounds

Access to a widely available model is unlikely to be a durable advantage. Competitors can buy it too.[2][4]

Advantage is more likely to come from the assets around the model:

- proprietary or difficult-to-recreate data;
- domain knowledge encoded into workflows and evaluations;
- customer access and feedback loops;
- people who can redesign work and judge outputs;
- reusable technology, security, and governance patterns;
- the ability to switch models or vendors without rebuilding everything; and
- partnerships that add capability without surrendering strategic control.

Some of these assets take time to build and produce little immediate revenue. Boards therefore need to distinguish an unproductive technology expense from a foundational investment that creates future options. Recent principles name data, infrastructure, talent, change management, partnerships, and M&A as foundational investments.[2] Other board frameworks ask boards to examine architecture, data readiness, vendor dependence, and the build-buy-partner decision as strategic capital-allocation questions.[3][4]

The central question is not “Which model are we using?” It is “What are we becoming unusually capable of doing?”

## 6. Treat risk management as scaling infrastructure

Risk management matters because value is difficult to sustain when an organization cannot trust, repeat, or defend what it has built.

PwC argues that well-designed risk governance can help organizations adopt AI faster.[4]

- a current inventory can reduce duplicated work and hidden dependencies;
- clear ownership can shorten decisions and escalation;
- risk tiers can give low-consequence experiments a faster route;
- approved data, security, and procurement patterns can reduce repeated review;
- testing and monitoring can catch failures while they are still reversible;
- incident processes can support customer and employee trust; and
- evidence can help a successful system pass from pilot to production.

The alternative is not freedom. It is delay, rework, fragmented buying, quiet misuse, and an eventual reaction when something breaks.

Governance should therefore be proportionate. A drafting assistant using approved public material does not need the same review as an agent that can transfer money or a system that influences hiring. A single heavy process for every use creates bottlenecks. No process creates unmanaged exposure. The board should expect management to build a fast path for bounded uses and stronger challenge for higher-risk or high-consequence ones.[4]

This is the connection between the two sides of the board's mandate. Strategy identifies where AI may create value. Risk management establishes the conditions under which the company can pursue that value with confidence.

## 7. Measure outcomes and learning

The board needs a scorecard that joins value, capability, and risk. Counting models, pilots, users, or tokens is not enough.

| Question | Illustrative evidence |
|---|---|
| **Is AI creating value?** | Revenue, retention, margin, cost-to-serve, cycle time, quality, customer experience |
| **Is it changing the business?** | Priority workflows redesigned, products changed, decisions improved, use cases operating at scale |
| **Are capabilities improving?** | Reusable data assets, critical roles filled, employee proficiency, adoption in target workflows, vendor optionality |
| **Are we learning?** | Assumptions tested, pilots stopped or scaled, time from experiment to decision, lessons reused across business units |
| **Can value be sustained responsibly?** | High-consequence systems independently tested, incidents and drift detected, remediation time, employee and customer trust |

One reporting framework connects AI reporting to strategic plans, investment, scaled use cases, business outcomes, redesigned processes, workforce readiness, and risk.[4] Another puts strategic, financial, and operational performance inside the board's AI-governance roadmap.[5]

Not every strategic investment will produce a clean short-term return. That makes staged evidence more important, not less. The board should know what management expected to learn, what it learned, and how the result changed the next decision.

## Five strategic questions for the broader board agenda

A board does not need to manage AI programs. It does need crisp answers to five questions:

1. **Where could AI materially improve or threaten how this company creates value?**
2. **Which opportunities will we lead, keep pace with, wait on, or leave—and why?**
3. **What capabilities must we own or build, and what can we buy or rent?**
4. **How will products, workflows, decision rights, and the workforce have to change?**
5. **What evidence would show that we are creating value responsibly enough to scale?**

These five strategic questions do not replace oversight of inventory, accountability, boundaries, monitoring, incidents, and assurance. They explain why those controls exist. The detailed companion guide, [AI Governance: Five Questions for the Board](/notes/ai-governance-board-note), provides five assurance questions for that work.

The board should not choose between opportunity and control. Opportunity without proportionate control can be difficult to scale responsibly. Control without opportunity protects a company that may be standing still.

## Limits

There is no universal AI strategy. The right choices depend on the company's sector, economics, data, customers, workforce, risk appetite, and jurisdictions. A board should challenge and oversee management rather than run individual deployments.

The sources below are institutional and practitioner guidance, not causal proof that a particular board structure or level of AI spending produces superior returns. Several contributors also advise or sell services to companies adopting AI. Together, the sources map a shared practitioner agenda. Convergence among adjacent advisory and governance organizations is not independent evidence that the recommended practices improve outcomes.
